How The Number Works

The formula behind every score in this book

Not degrees, not effort, not joint range. Friction is a weighted sum of five categorical comparisons between two poses — the only inputs are fields already in data/poses/*.json.

This is the full arithmetic behind every seam in Part II — the book itself moves straight to the seams.

Formula .35·contact + .25·orientation + .20·height + .10·spine + .10·plane
TermRuleWeight
ContactJaccard distance between the two base_of_support sets — 1 minus (shared parts ÷ total distinct parts)0.35
Orientation1.0 if orientation differs; 0.5 if orientation holds but level differs; else 00.25
Heightcog_height position on the ladder floor·low·mid·high, steps apart ÷ 30.20
Spine1 if spinal_action differs, else 00.10
Plane1 if plane differs; 0.5 if either pose is multi; else 00.10

Weights sum to exactly 1.00. Tiers: <0.34 easy, <0.67 costly, else abrupt — the words already printed in every page margin.

Across all 4,422 ordered pairs in the 67-pose library, 36 score exactly 0.000 and 102 score exactly 1.000 — both ends are real pages, not theoretical extremes. Median across all pairs: 0.667.

Below chance

The 54 real seams across the three built-in flows average 0.335 — roughly half the 0.624 mean of an arbitrary pair. Teachers already sequence at well below the cost of chance, without ever seeing this number.

Whether these five weights are the right five, in the right proportion, is a judgment call, not a computed fact — it's the first question in the feedback guide.

Why This Is Co-Authorship

The method is the credibility

The model can measure a shape. It cannot tell you what to do with what it finds — that call needs years on a mat, not a schema.

The data is real, not a mockup

Every page comes from an app teachers actually use, and every number is computed, not invented for this pitch. What's missing is the half only a teacher can supply.

Atlases are made by more than one hand

No historical atlas was drawn by one person — surveyors, engravers, editors, all credited. Cartographer and teacher is the honest structure here.

A shared instrument, not a writing credit

The scoring above is a first draft of something yoga doesn't have. Co-deciding which questions belong, and what the numbers mean, is a career artifact.

The Corpus

Straight counts across the library

67 poses in data/poses/, tagged against the Atlas schema (docs/krama-atlas.md). Not opinions about yoga — a description of this specific, growing library.

43 of 67 poses move in the sagittal plane alone. Only 7 are coronal, 4 transverse. Yoga, as practiced here, is overwhelmingly a forward-and-back art.

Finding A — Computed, Reproducible

The families nobody computed

Single-link clustering over the full 67×67 friction matrix at threshold 0.20 yields 14 families, no categories asserted in advance. 13 of the 14 are pure on a single field: body_position (standing / supine / kneeling / seated / prone). Meanwhile the categories teachers plan by — type_tags like backbend, forward-fold, hip-opener, twist — are smeared almost evenly across every cluster.

Method single-link clustering, friction ≤ 0.20, over data/poses/*.json
The claim

Teachers plan by morphology — what the spine is doing. Flow is governed by posture — what the floor is holding and which way the body faces. Those are two different maps of the same 67 poses, and sequencing has mostly been taught with the wrong one.

Finding B — Terra Incognita

The map has blank regions

27 of the 67 poses collide into 12 groups the friction engine cannot tell apart at all — identical on every one of the five geometry terms it currently reads, so the computed friction between them is exactly 0.000.

Worked case mountain → tree, score 0.000

One is a resting stance, the other a single-leg balance that asks for real attention — and on the five terms the engine reads today, they are the same pose. The cause is precise and fixable: base_of_support records which parts touch the floor, never how many — one foot and two feet are both just ["feet"]. The missing resolution is exactly the two fields already flagged unbuilt in docs/pose-model-review.html: joint_config and laterality-as-count.

Finding C — The Arc, In Full

What the book's seam-by-seam line chart actually depends on

The line chart printed in book-pages.html plots all 33 seams of the heart-openers class in order — seam position on the x-axis, not elapsed minutes. That choice, and the phase regions named along the axis, carry two dependencies that don't survive being printed as a bare line. Both are stated here in full.

Why seam position, not minutes the flow's authored measures total 35.3 minutes, not 60, and 26 of 33 seams fall in the first 7 authored minutes

A true elapsed-minutes x-axis would print a false clock and crush most of the seam-to-seam variation against the left edge. Plotting seam position instead is still the literal shape of the class — the phase boundaries just aren't evenly spaced in wall-clock time behind it.

Attribution rule a seam is counted toward the phase of the item it enters, not the one it leaves

Swap the rule — attribute each seam to the earlier item's phase instead — and the phase boundaries drawn on the chart shift: the closing seam (0.867) moves into Cooldown, and Cooldown's own average rises from 0.379 to 0.486. The rule is a modeling choice, not a fact about the flow.

Seams behind each phase region Opening n=1 · Warm-up n=16 · Standing n=7 · Peak n=3 · Cooldown n=5 · Closing n=1

Two of the six regions are a single seam. The chart plots every seam individually rather than averaging into six bars, which is exactly what keeps a one-seam region from reading as equivalent to a sixteen-seam one.

The more durable finding sits underneath the line's overall shape: seams that stay within one body_position average 0.159 (n=30, across all three flows). Seams that cross a body-position boundary average 0.556 (n=24) — 3.50×. This is consistent with Finding A above (13 of 14 clusters are body_position-pure) and is very likely the real mechanism behind the chart's shape: stretches that stay in one posture family read as "quiet," stretches that change posture read as "loud," largely independent of which phase they're labeled.

Two confounds checked and ruled out

(1) Repeated poses within a flow do not explain the cheap middle: deduplicating repeated seams moves the Warm-up average only from 0.270 to 0.292. (2) Missing data does not explain any zero-cost seam: all 67 poses carry all six geometry fields the engine reads, so no seam scores low because a field was blank.

All three flows in data/flows/ are marked isBuiltIn: true with identical synthetic timestamps — they were composed for this library, not pulled from a teacher's real class history — worth saying plainly rather than calling them "already-taught classes."

A Question Worth Asking — Answered

Do kinespheres already fill the gap?

This schema borrowed Laban's vocabulary for the space around a body at rest — plane, level, zone — which is a different thing from Labanotation itself, which does notate movement through time and has been adapted for physiotherapy, swimming, and dance forms outside yoga. The fair question isn't whether Labanotation works; it's whether these three borrowed fields, on their own, already amount to one. Computed answer: no, and the corpus shows exactly why.

Method distinct (plane, level, zone) triples across all 67 poses

The claim

These three kinesphere fields are a coordinate system for one body at rest, not a record of what changed between two. Adding them doesn't repair Finding B either — mountain, tree, and dangling share one triple, so they add no resolution exactly where the engine is already blind. The field actually worth developing is zone — near 26 · far 25 · mid-reach 16 — the best-distributed of the three and the only one describing how far the body reaches from its own centre; it is currently unweighted in the v0.1 friction formula. What makes this notation-capable isn't the kinesphere fields — it's that friction is already a difference operator.

Errata

A discrepancy, for the record

Finding D

docs/pose-model-review.html headlines plank → down-dog at 0.15, "very smooth." The shipped engine computes 0.417, tier 2 — because down-dog is tagged orientation: inverted against plank's prone, firing the orientation term at full weight.

The prose ("no prone↔supine flip") is true; the engine's orientation term isn't prone↔supine-only. Either the review doc oversells the pair, or down-dog's orientation tag deserves a second look. Left uncorrected here — see the open questions below.
